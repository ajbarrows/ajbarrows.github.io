\bsheading*{Personal Statement}

My research focuses on understanding brain-behavior relationships through computational and predictive modeling, with particular emphasis on cognitive control processes and substance use behaviors. I am a Postdoctoral Associate in the Department of Psychiatry at the University of Vermont. I completed my PhD in Complex Systems and Data Science at the University of Vermont in 2026, supported as an NIH T32 predoctoral fellow in the Neuroimaging REsearch VErmont (NERVE) Lab, where I developed expertise in applying cognitive process models and multivariate, machine-learning-based approaches to reveal meaningful associations between neural activation patterns and behavioral outcomes. My work primarily uses data from large-scale developmental studies, including the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study, to investigate how the cognitive mechanisms underlying behavioral tasks relate to brain function.

My research interests span the intersection of computational neuroscience, cognitive psychology, and public health. I am particularly interested in how individual differences in brain function and cognition relate to risk factors for psychopathology and substance use. Before beginning doctoral training, I spent six years as a research assistant and data analyst at the Vermont Center on Behavior and Health, where I contributed to multi-site randomized clinical trials of reduced nicotine content cigarettes among vulnerable populations. Through my training in the NERVE Lab, I gained extensive experience in neuroimaging data analysis, statistical learning, and computational modeling, as well as a deep commitment to open science and open-source software, including serving as a Teaching Fellow for the ABCD-ReproNim course on reproducible neuroimaging.

My long-term career goals center on using robust, reproducible analytical techniques to understand the neurocognitive mechanisms underlying behavioral health outcomes, with the ultimate aim of informing intervention strategies. I am committed to advancing methodological best practices and promoting the adoption of reproducible research workflows that can be easily shared, validated, and extended by the broader scientific community.

\bsheading*{Contributions to Science}
\begin{contributions}
	\contribution{Computational Modeling of Cognitive Control and Brain-Behavior Associations}
	Characterizing associations between individual differences in brain activity and behavior remains a central challenge in functional neuroimaging. My dissertation work applied formal computational models of cognitive processes to the ABCD Study stop-signal task, decomposing behavioral performance into mechanistic parameters and relating them to task-related brain activation using a machine-learning-based predictive modeling framework. In a sample of more than 6,000 children aged 9--11, these model parameters showed substantially stronger associations with fMRI activation than conventional empirically derived performance measures, and pointed to evidence accumulation for the go process and attentional lapses (``trigger failure'') as the mechanisms most closely tied to brain function. Relationships with phenotypic measures of inhibition, impulsivity, and cognition further supported the construct validity of the model. Building on this approach, I have collaborated on work using multivariate ``neural signatures'' of working memory to study how screen time activities relate to longitudinal brain development in adolescents, demonstrating that multivariate models can help resolve individual differences that mass-univariate analyses miss.

	\contribution{Smoking Reduction and Cessation}
	Reducing the number of cigarettes smoked per day is a common step toward quitting, but which patterns of reduction predict eventual cessation has been unclear. Using secondary analysis of longitudinal randomized clinical trial data, I applied latent class trajectory modeling to identify distinct patterns of smoking reduction and quantified their association with later cessation. This work informs the design of reduction-based cessation interventions by identifying the early reduction patterns most predictive of quitting, and has been presented to the Vermont Department of Health to inform state tobacco cessation benefit programs.

	\contribution{Reduced Nicotine Content Cigarettes in Vulnerable Populations}
	As a research assistant and data analyst at the Vermont Center on Behavior and Health, I contributed to a series of multi-site randomized clinical trials examining the effects of reduced nicotine content cigarettes among populations at elevated risk for smoking and its consequences, including individuals with psychiatric conditions, opioid use disorder, and socioeconomic disadvantage. This work demonstrated that reducing nicotine content decreased cigarette consumption across these vulnerable populations, providing evidence relevant to a potential FDA nicotine product standard. I also contributed to related studies of behavioral economic demand for cigarettes, co-use of cannabis, and changes in smoking behavior during the COVID-19 pandemic.
\end{contributions}
